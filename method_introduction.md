# Toward Geometry Invocation for Spatial Reasoning

## Introduction Draft

Vision-language models have made remarkable progress in connecting visual observations with language. Yet spatial reasoning remains a persistent weakness. In many recent geometry-aware VLMs, this limitation is addressed by injecting 3D geometry tokens into an existing VLM pipeline. A typical design first extracts 2D visual tokens with a vision encoder, extracts 3D geometry tokens with a separate geometry encoder, and then fuses the two token streams before sending them to the language model. GeoSR further improves this recipe by masking part of the 2D visual tokens during training and using a gated fusion module, thereby forcing the model to consult geometry when appearance-only shortcuts are insufficient.

This direction is important, but it also exposes a deeper question: what role should geometry play in a VLM? Should geometry be treated as an additional information source appended to an already formed visual interpretation, or should it participate in the formation of the representation used for reasoning? Existing geometry-token fusion methods mostly operate after each modality-specific encoder has already produced its own explanation of the scene. In this setting, 2D visual tokens and 3D geometry tokens are not raw views of the same world, but encoder-dependent interpretations shaped by different pretraining objectives. Fusing them at this stage may therefore be closer to representation alignment or information mixing than to genuine multimodal reasoning.

An analogy from written language helps clarify this concern. Suppose the same phenomenon can be written in simplified Chinese, traditional Chinese, seal script, or oracle bone script. These writing systems are visually different, but they can refer to the same underlying meaning. If text tokens are like simplified Chinese characters and 2D visual tokens are like seal script, then introducing 3D geometry tokens is like introducing oracle bone script: it is related to the same world, but it follows a different representational convention and cannot be fully understood through naive character-level fusion. What makes these forms mutually interpretable is not simply their final glyph shapes, but a more underlying shared structure, such as pronunciation, meaning, and context. In this analogy, pinyin is not the final written form; it is an intermediate latent code that can connect different surface forms before meaning is finalized.

This motivates a different view of geometry-aware VLMs. Instead of asking only how to fuse final 2D and 3D tokens, we should ask whether there exists a more modality-agnostic latent representation before each encoder commits to its final semantic interpretation. The world or scene is the underlying phenomenon. Text tokens, 2D visual tokens, and 3D geometry tokens are different symbolic or perceptual descriptions of that world. If fusion happens only after each branch has independently completed its interpretation, then the model is forced to reconcile final decisions. Spatial reasoning may instead require interaction at a pre-decision stage, where multiple possible interpretations are still preserved.

Recent work such as SpatialStack provides an important step in this direction. Its core observation is that different layers of a geometry encoder contain spatial information at different granularities: shallow layers tend to preserve local and fine-grained geometric cues, while deeper layers capture more global structure and semantic context. SpatialStack also shows that naively aggregating multi-level geometry features into the visual branch does not necessarily yield complementary gains; instead, it may introduce feature interference. To address this, SpatialStack injects geometry features from different depths into different decoder layers of the language model, forming a hierarchical geometry-language fusion strategy.

However, this finding also reveals a further problem. Even if multi-level geometry features contain complementary spatial information, existing methods still largely rely on pre-designed and static fusion policies. The correspondence between geometry layers and language layers is manually specified in advance, and the model receives multi-level geometry through a fixed injection pattern. In other words, the model may access hierarchical geometry, but it does not truly learn whether the current question requires geometry, what granularity of geometry is needed, or when geometry should participate in the reasoning process. The feature interference observed by SpatialStack further suggests that different geometric representations are not naturally additive; their usefulness depends on the task, the visual content, and the current reasoning state.

This shifts the research question from geometry fusion to geometry selection and eventually to geometry invocation. Rather than asking only how to inject multiple geometry layers, we ask whether a model can actively decide when to call geometry, whether geometry is necessary at all, which geometry layer is most suitable, and at what stage of reasoning it should be introduced. This distinction is crucial. A fixed hierarchical fusion design still treats geometry as a passively injected resource. A geometry invocation mechanism would instead treat geometry as an actively selected source of evidence, conditioned on the question, the scene, and the model's evolving internal state.

The practical challenge is that training a fully unified 2D-3D-language foundation model from scratch is prohibitively expensive. A more feasible research path is to reuse existing pretrained encoders, but avoid relying exclusively on their final-layer outputs. Instead, we can probe and compare intermediate representations from the 2D visual encoder and the 3D geometry encoder, and study when and where geometry should be introduced. In the simplest controlled experiment, one can keep the same GeoSR-style masking and gated fusion mechanism, but replace the default geometry tokens with tokens from earlier or middle layers of the geometry encoder. If middle-layer geometry tokens consistently outperform final-layer tokens under the same training data, evaluation benchmark, and fusion design, this would support the claim that fusion timing and representation stage matter.

This leads to a broader research direction: adaptive geometry invocation through pre-interpretation representations. The model should not merely decide how much 2D and 3D information to mix; it should decide whether geometry is needed, which layer of geometry is useful, and at which stage geometry should enter the representation-forming process. Such a framework moves beyond treating geometry as an auxiliary side channel. It treats geometry as a participant in constructing the latent scene representation used for reasoning.

The initial goal is therefore not to propose a fully general multimodal tokenizer immediately, but to validate the key premise behind invocation: different spatial questions may require different geometry representations, and fixed final-layer geometry may not be the optimal evidence source. A controlled static spatial reasoning experiment can test this premise by comparing default GeoSR-style fusion with fusion using early, middle, late, and final geometry-layer tokens. If the gains concentrate on geometry-sensitive tasks such as distance estimation, relative direction, room size, and route planning, the result would suggest that spatial reasoning benefits from selecting geometry at the appropriate representation stage rather than passively injecting a single predetermined token stream.

In short, the question is not only how to make VLMs use geometry, nor only how to fuse multi-level geometry. GeoSR asks how to prevent geometry tokens from being ignored. SpatialStack asks how multi-level geometry should be fused. The proposed direction asks how a model can actively decide when, whether, and which geometry to invoke. This reframes geometry from a passively injected feature stream into an actively selected component of spatial reasoning.
