# From Geometry Fusion to Geometry Invocation for Spatial Reasoning

## Introduction Draft

Vision-language models (VLMs) have made remarkable progress in connecting visual observations with language, yet reliable spatial reasoning remains difficult. A common response is to augment a VLM with a pretrained geometry encoder: a vision encoder extracts appearance-oriented visual tokens, a geometry encoder extracts implicit 3D tokens, and a fusion module combines the two streams before language reasoning. This paradigm provides the model with geometric evidence, but providing geometry does not guarantee that the model will use it. When 2D appearance offers an easier shortcut, the geometry branch may contribute little and can even hurt performance.

GeoSR directly exposes and addresses this failure mode. Its Geometry-Unleashing Masking suppresses part of the 2D visual evidence during training, encouraging the model to consult geometry rather than rely exclusively on appearance. Its Geometry-Guided Fusion then uses a token- and channel-wise gate to regulate the relative contributions of visual and geometric features. In dynamic scenes, GeoSR further retrieves question-relevant geometric locations through a QFormer-style mechanism. These designs make geometry harder to ignore and more selectively used in space. Nevertheless, the geometric representation itself is still taken from a predetermined encoder depth, and its role in the language model is governed by a fixed fusion architecture. GeoSR therefore answers an important question--how to make geometry matter--while leaving open which form of geometry should matter and when it should enter reasoning.

This distinction is important because geometry encoders do not produce a single uniform type of spatial evidence. Their intermediate layers form a hierarchy of representations shaped by different receptive fields and levels of abstraction. A useful analogy is the relationship among simplified Chinese, traditional Chinese, seal script, and oracle bone script. These surface forms may describe the same underlying meaning, but a direct character-level alignment is not always sufficient. Their correspondence becomes clearer through intermediate structure, such as pronunciation, composition, meaning, and context. Likewise, visual and geometric tokens are not raw copies of the same scene. They are encoder-dependent interpretations produced under different pretraining objectives. Their final-layer features may already emphasize different abstractions, making late fusion a reconciliation of completed interpretations rather than an interaction during representation formation.

SpatialStack provides strong evidence for this hierarchical view. Its layer-wise analysis shows that relatively shallow geometry features preserve fine-grained local structure, whereas deeper features increasingly encode global structure and semantic context. Correspondingly, shallow or middle geometry layers better support low-level tasks such as depth and distance perception, while deeper layers better support high-level tasks such as cross-view spatial relations and route planning. SpatialStack also finds that naively aggregating features from multiple geometry layers in the visual pathway creates feature interference rather than consistent synergy. It therefore injects geometry features from selected encoder depths into successive early layers of the language decoder, establishing a progressive geometry-language hierarchy.

However, SpatialStack uses the same manually specified correspondence for every sample: selected geometry layers are always injected into predetermined language layers in a fixed order. This design establishes that representation depth and injection stage matter, but it does not determine them conditionally. A depth question, a route-planning question, and a question answerable from appearance alone all receive the same geometry stack. The model cannot explicitly choose to skip geometry, select only the representation granularity relevant to the current question, or defer geometry until a later reasoning stage. Consequently, hierarchical fusion remains a static policy even when the utility of geometry is task-dependent.

These observations motivate a shift from **geometry fusion** to **geometry invocation**. Geometry fusion asks how a given geometric representation should be combined with visual tokens. Geometry invocation asks a broader set of decisions:

1. **Whether to invoke:** Does the current question require external geometric evidence, or are visual-language features already sufficient?
2. **What to invoke:** Which geometry-encoder depth, or which small combination of depths, provides the appropriate granularity of evidence?
3. **When to invoke:** At which language-decoder stage does that evidence become useful for the model's current reasoning state?

Under this view, geometry is not a feature stream that must always be injected. It is a structured source of evidence that can be queried when needed. The invocation decision should be conditioned not only on the input question and scene, but also on the model's evolving hidden state. This separates the proposed direction from GeoSR's spatially selective gating and SpatialStack's fixed depth-to-stage mapping: the target is joint, question-conditioned selection over geometry necessity, representation depth, and reasoning stage.

Training a fully unified 2D-3D-language foundation model from scratch would be prohibitively expensive. A practical first step is therefore to reuse frozen pretrained vision and geometry encoders while exposing intermediate geometry features as a small bank of candidate evidence. A lightweight router can assign weights over a candidate set such as {no geometry, shallow geometry, middle geometry, deep geometry}, conditioned on the question, visual context, and decoder state. The selected evidence can then be injected only at the decoder stages where the router predicts it to be useful. This formulation allows geometry invocation to be studied without changing the underlying foundation encoders.

Before introducing the full router, a controlled layer-probing experiment is needed to verify the premise in the target framework. Under the same training data, benchmark, masking strategy, and GeoSR-style gated fusion, the default geometry representation can be replaced with features from different encoder depths. This experiment is not itself the final contribution--SpatialStack has already demonstrated task-dependent layer behavior under conventional geometry-vision fusion--but it tests whether the same hierarchy persists when shortcut suppression and adaptive visual-geometry gating are present. It also identifies a suitable candidate layer set for subsequent invocation experiments.

The central hypothesis is stronger than the claim that an intermediate geometry layer may outperform the final layer on average. We hypothesize that **there is no universally optimal geometry representation**: the utility of a geometry layer depends jointly on the spatial question and the stage of reasoning. Low-level measurement tasks may benefit from geometry that preserves local boundaries and metric detail; high-level relational or planning tasks may require more globally organized representations; non-geometric questions may benefit from invoking no geometry at all. A successful invocation mechanism should therefore improve accuracy while producing interpretable, task-dependent routing patterns rather than collapsing to one geometry layer for every sample.

This hypothesis suggests three levels of empirical validation. First, layer probing should reproduce distinct task-wise preferences across geometry depths. Second, a question-conditioned selector should outperform both single-layer fusion and fixed multi-level fusion under comparable parameter and compute budgets. Third, a reasoning-stage-aware router should improve upon a fixed SpatialStack-style mapping and reveal different invocation trajectories for perception, relational reasoning, and planning. Evaluation should report not only aggregate accuracy, but also per-task gains, layer-selection distributions, no-geometry decisions, routing sparsity, and computational overhead.

In summary, GeoSR asks how to prevent geometry from being ignored, and SpatialStack asks how hierarchical geometry can be aligned with the language hierarchy. The proposed direction asks how a model can decide whether geometry is needed, which geometric representation is appropriate, and when it should participate in reasoning. This reframes geometry from a passively supplied auxiliary stream into an actively invoked source of spatial evidence.
